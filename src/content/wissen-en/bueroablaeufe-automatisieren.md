<p>Workflows that recur regularly, follow clear rules and are already digital are easy to automate. The typical candidates in everyday office work: sorting invoices from the inbox into the right folder, transferring data between two systems, sending appointment confirmations, generating recurring reports and filling in documents from templates.</p>

<p>Anything that needs case-by-case judgment, changes often or only happens a few times a year is hard to automate. The effort to set it up and maintain it never pays off.</p>

## The three-question test
<p>Before you think about technology, check a workflow against these three questions:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>How often?</strong> Daily or several times a week almost always pays off. Monthly pays off if the task takes a long time. A few times a year practically never does.</li>
<li><strong>How clear-cut?</strong> Can you explain the workflow to someone in five sentences so they can do it without asking questions? Then it's rule-based enough. If your explanation starts with "it depends", it gets tricky.</li>
<li><strong>How stable?</strong> A workflow that changes every three months has to be updated each time. With frequent change, that can cost more than doing it by hand.</li>
</ol>

<div class="keypoint">
<p><strong>Do the math:</strong> a task that takes ten minutes a day adds up to around 40 hours a year. At an internal rate of €40 an hour, that's €1,600 a year for a single routine task. That's usually the point at which automation becomes worthwhile.</p>
</div>

## The best candidates in everyday office work
<h3>Incoming invoices and filing</h3>

<p>Invoices arrive by email, get opened, renamed, moved to the right folder and forwarded to accounting. Five steps, several times a day. With automation, the system recognizes the invoice, reads the supplier and date, names the file according to a fixed scheme and files it in the right place.</p>

<p>The side effect is often more valuable than the time saved: every invoice is named the same way and stored in the same place. Finding last March's invoice then takes seconds instead of a quarter of an hour.</p>

<h3>Data transfer between systems</h3>

<p>The classic case: the same data is entered twice, once in the online shop and once in the inventory system. Or customer details from the contact form are copied into the CRM by hand. Double entry doesn't just cost time; it creates discrepancies between the systems.</p>

<h3>Appointment confirmations and reminders</h3>

<p>A confirmation after booking, a reminder the day before, a follow-up after the appointment. Especially in medical practices, law firms and workshops, this noticeably reduces no-shows, and every no-show is revenue lost immediately.</p>

<h3>Recurring reports</h3>

<p>If you copy figures from several sources into a spreadsheet every month, format them and send them out, you have an ideal candidate for automation: clear sources, a fixed format, a fixed date.</p>

<h3>Documents from templates</h3>

<p>Quotes, contracts, job sheets, confirmations: anywhere a document is created from existing data and a fixed template. It saves time and prevents the version where the quote still shows the previous customer's name.</p>

## Where automation doesn't work
<p>Being honest here saves money:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Workflows nobody can describe clearly.</strong> If three employees give three different explanations, the process isn't ready for automation. Clarify first, then automate.</li>
<li><strong>Decisions that require judgment.</strong> Whether a goodwill request is approved is decided by a person. The path to that decision (collecting, preparing and presenting documents) can be automated; the decision itself can't.</li>
<li><strong>Rare tasks.</strong> The year-end close that takes four hours once a year rarely justifies two days of development.</li>
<li><strong>Processes in systems without an interface.</strong> There are workarounds using screen automation, but they're error-prone and break with every update. Sometimes switching systems is the better answer.</li>
</ul>

## How an automation project works
<ol>
<li><strong>Document the workflow.</strong> Someone watches and writes down what actually happens, not what's in the manual. The two almost always differ.</li>
<li><strong>Collect the special cases.</strong> What happens with a credit note? An invoice without an order number? An unknown supplier? The special cases determine the effort, not the normal case.</li>
<li><strong>Define and document the rules.</strong> Including what should happen when the automation doesn't know what to do. Usually: hand over to a person, don't guess.</li>
<li><strong>Build it and test it in parallel.</strong> For one to two weeks, both run side by side and the results are compared.</li>
<li><strong>Hand over and document.</strong> Someone in the business needs to know where to look when something gets stuck.</li>
</ol>

<p>The second step is where projects fail. If you only automate the normal case and ignore the special cases, you build a system that constantly needs manual work and therefore saves nothing.</p>

## Two rules from practice
<p><strong>Start small.</strong> One workflow, cleanly automated and proven in daily operation, is worth more than a big project that tries to solve everything at once. The first workflow also brings the insights that make the second one much faster.</p>

<p><strong>Don't automate what should be abolished.</strong> Sometimes documenting a process reveals that a step only exists because it always has. Automation doesn't make an unnecessary step useful; it just makes it unnecessary faster.</p>
