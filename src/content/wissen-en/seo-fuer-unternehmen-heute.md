<p>For small and mid-sized businesses, four areas decide visibility on Google: a technically clean website, a fully maintained Google Business Profile, content that answers specific search questions rather than general terms, and mentions on other websites. In that order.</p>

<p>Everything beyond that (fine-tuning headings, keyword density, checking rankings daily) rarely pays back the effort it takes for a small business.</p>

## 1. The technical foundations
<p>Without clean technology, even the best text won't help much. These points are the minimum standard today, not optimization:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>HTTPS throughout,</strong> with automatic redirects from unencrypted requests.</li>
<li><strong>A mobile version identical to the desktop version.</strong> Google evaluates the mobile version only. Whatever is missing there doesn't exist for search.</li>
<li><strong>Loading speed.</strong> The point where most website-builder and overloaded WordPress sites lose out. Large, uncompressed images are the most common cause.</li>
<li><strong>Readable URLs.</strong> <em>/insights/managed-it-cost</em> instead of <em>/?p=42</em>.</li>
<li><strong>A sitemap</strong> and a working robots.txt.</li>
<li><strong>No duplicate content</strong> under several URLs.</li>
</ul>

<p>This list can be worked through in a day, and it pays off permanently. That makes it the first step, not the last.</p>

## 2. The Google Business Profile
<p>For businesses serving a local area, the business profile is often more important than the website. It decides visibility in the map results, the block that appears at the very top for searches with a local angle.</p>

<p>What counts:</p>

<ul>
<li>Complete and exactly matching name, address and phone number, including in every directory you're listed in. Inconsistencies weaken the match.</li>
<li>The right categories, as specific as possible.</li>
<li>Current opening hours, including public holidays.</li>
<li>Real photos of your business.</li>
<li>Reviews, and replies to them, including the critical ones.</li>
</ul>

<p>The effort is small, and the impact on local search is bigger than that of most other measures.</p>

<div class="keypoint">
<p><strong>Where small businesses can actually win:</strong> not with generic terms like "IT service provider", but with specific searches like "managed IT law firm Frankfurt cost". Less search volume, but far less competition, and people looking for exactly what you offer.</p>
</div>

## 3. Content that answers specific questions
<p>The most effective approach for small businesses is unspectacular: write down what customers actually ask you. Not what you'd like to say about yourself.</p>

<p>In practice: for two weeks, note which questions come up in first conversations. Each of these questions is a search term. For the most common ones, write a page that answers the question completely, with concrete numbers wherever possible.</p>

<p>Instead of lots of isolated articles, it pays to group them: one in-depth main page on the core topic, plus several detail pages on specific aspects, all linked to each other. That tells Google you cover the whole topic, not just a single keyword.</p>

## 4. Mentions beyond your own website
<p>What others write about you counts for more than what you write about yourself. Realistically achievable for small businesses:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Industry directories and chambers of commerce.</strong> With exactly the same details as everywhere else.</li>
<li><strong>Local press.</strong> Every opening, award, partnership or donation can be worth an article, and therefore a mention.</li>
<li><strong>Expert articles on other websites.</strong> One or two per quarter is a realistic goal.</li>
<li><strong>Partners and customers.</strong> People who work with you may be happy to mention you; you just have to ask.</li>
</ul>

<p>Quality matters more than quantity. One mention on a relevant, reputable site is worth more than fifty entries in link directories nobody reads.</p>

## What changed in 2026
<p>Three shifts worth knowing about:</p>

<p><strong>A growing share of searches ends without a click.</strong> Google increasingly answers information-seeking questions directly in its AI Overviews. If you're named as a source there, you still win: your brand becomes visible even without a visit.</p>

<p><strong>Trust signals have become more important.</strong> Google has explicitly stressed the importance of transparent authorship. Complete legal information, an about page with real people and articles with named authors all contribute.</p>

<p><strong>FAQ markup no longer brings special display.</strong> The expandable FAQ results have been discontinued. The content is still worthwhile; the expectation of a prettier search result isn't.</p>

## What you can safely ignore
<ul>
<li><strong>Checking rankings daily.</strong> Positions fluctuate; it means nothing. A monthly look is enough.</li>
<li><strong>Keyword density.</strong> A concept from the early 2000s.</li>
<li><strong>Offers of bought links.</strong> Useless at best, harmful at worst.</li>
<li><strong>Tools before the foundations are in place.</strong> An expensive analytics tool will just show you in more detail that your loading time is too slow.</li>
</ul>

## A realistic order for the first few months
<ol>
<li><strong>Month 1:</strong> check and fix the technical foundations. Set up Google Search Console. Fill in your business profile completely.</li>
<li><strong>Month 2:</strong> collect customer questions and answer the three most common ones on their own pages.</li>
<li><strong>Month 3:</strong> make your directory listings consistent and actively ask for reviews.</li>
<li><strong>From month 4:</strong> keep answering more questions regularly. One article a month is enough: consistency beats intensity.</li>
</ol>

<p>The first measurable changes usually appear after three to six months. Anyone promising faster results is either selling ads or something that will cause problems later.</p>
