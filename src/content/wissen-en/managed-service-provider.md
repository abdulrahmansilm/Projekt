<p>A managed service provider (MSP) is an IT service company that looks after a business's technology on an ongoing basis and charges a fixed monthly fee for it, rather than billing hour by hour. The MSP monitors systems continuously, installs updates, backs up data, fixes problems and is the point of contact for every IT question. In other words, the customer isn't buying individual repairs, but IT that simply works.</p>

<p>That sounds like a billing detail, but it's a fundamentally different business model. And it changes how the provider makes money.</p>

## How it differs from a traditional IT company
<p>A traditional break-fix IT company works on call. Something breaks, you phone, someone comes over, and you get a bill for the time spent. The more that breaks, the better business is. Nobody builds in problems on purpose, but in this model there's simply no financial incentive to prevent them in the first place.</p>

<p>With an MSP, it's the other way round. The fee is fixed, no matter how often something goes wrong. Every problem avoided is profit for the provider and less downtime for you. Almost everything else follows from this one change: monitoring instead of waiting, scheduled updates instead of updates after an incident, documentation instead of knowledge locked in individual heads.</p>

<div class="keypoint">
<p><strong>In short:</strong> a traditional IT company earns money by solving problems. An MSP earns money when problems never happen.</p>
</div>

## What an MSP actually takes care of
<p>The scope varies from provider to provider, but there's a core you'll find almost everywhere:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Round-the-clock monitoring:</strong> software on every device reports when a hard drive fails, storage fills up or a service stops responding, often before anyone in the office notices.</li>
<li><strong>Patch management:</strong> operating system and software updates are managed and installed centrally, rather than left to the employee who has been dismissing the notification for three weeks.</li>
<li><strong>Antivirus and endpoint security:</strong> managed centrally, with a clear overview of which devices are actually protected.</li>
<li><strong>Data backup:</strong> automated, with regular restore tests.</li>
<li><strong>Ticket-based support:</strong> requests are logged, prioritized and handled in a traceable way.</li>
<li><strong>Documentation:</strong> which devices exist, which accounts, which contracts, which quirks. It sounds dull, but in an emergency it decides whether recovery takes hours or days.</li>
</ul>

<p>Many MSPs also offer additional services: sourcing and setting up hardware, Microsoft 365 administration, email security, VPN access for remote work and server management.</p>

## What an MSP costs
<p>Pricing is usually per device per month, less often per user. The price depends on what's included in the package and how quickly the provider responds. At Selim-IT, managed IT starts at €30 per device per month. That includes the essentials: monitoring, troubleshooting, maintenance, ticket support, patch management, antivirus, ongoing documentation and a monthly report.</p>

<p>For a business with ten workstations, that means a modest three-figure amount each month: predictable, without surprises, and well below the cost of a part-time IT employee of your own.</p>

## When the model pays off
<p>The rule of thumb is less about headcount than about dependency. A four-person business where a server outage brings all revenue to a halt needs more support than a twenty-person business whose staff mostly work without IT.</p>

<p>Clear signs that outsourced IT support is worth it:</p>

<ul>
<li>Nobody in-house is truly responsible for IT, just the colleague who "knows a bit about computers".</li>
<li>Nobody can say off the top of their head whether last night's backup ran.</li>
<li>Problems sit for days because someone first has to find the time.</li>
<li>IT costs fluctuate widely and are impossible to plan.</li>
<li>A day of downtime would cost noticeable money.</li>
</ul>

<p>If two or more of these apply, the question is no longer whether outsourced support makes sense, only how much of it you need.</p>

## When an MSP isn't the right choice
<p>To be honest, the model doesn't fit everywhere. If you have your own IT department and only buy in specialist knowledge now and then, project-based billing works better. If you work with nothing more than two laptops and cloud services, with no server and no special requirements, you don't need ongoing support; a contact for the occasional issue is enough.</p>

<p>And if you mainly want to complete a one-off project, such as a new website or automating a workflow, you should be able to order exactly that. At Selim-IT, projects like these can be billed once based on effort, with ongoing support as an option.</p>

## How to recognize a good provider
<p>Four points that quickly bring clarity in a first conversation:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Guaranteed response times in the contract.</strong> "We'll get back to you quickly" isn't a commitment. Specific hours, graded by urgency, are.</li>
<li><strong>A clear scope of services.</strong> What's included, and what's billed separately? A written breakdown makes proposals comparable and costs predictable.</li>
<li><strong>Transparent documentation.</strong> Ask whether you can access the documentation of your own systems at any time. The answer tells you a lot.</li>
<li><strong>Contract terms that suit you.</strong> Short notice periods give you flexibility; longer terms often come with a discount. What matters is that you have the choice. Selim-IT contracts can be cancelled monthly, and an annual contract is an optional extra.</li>
</ol>

## Switching to an MSP
<p>The most common worry is the effort of switching. In practice, a switch happens in three phases: taking stock of existing devices, accounts and contracts; gradually bringing the systems under management; and then fine-tuning. For typical business sizes this takes two to three weeks, and day-to-day operations continue throughout.</p>

<p>The critical point is rarely the technology; it's the handover: who has which passwords, where the licenses are, which contracts run where. A good MSP will put most of its effort into exactly this at the start, and that's time well invested.</p>
