<p>An AI phone assistant answers incoming calls automatically, holds a conversation in natural language, captures what the caller needs and passes it on in a structured way: by email, into your ticket system or as a calendar entry. Unlike a keypad menu, it understands freely spoken sentences and can ask follow-up questions.</p>

<p>It doesn't replace expert advice or a sales conversation. It makes sure no call goes unanswered.</p>

## The problem it solves
<p>In most small businesses, the phone rings exactly when everyone is busy. The call goes to voicemail, the caller hangs up and calls the next company on the list. Anyone who has ever tried to reach a tradesperson knows both sides of this problem.</p>

<p>An unanswered call isn't just a missed message; it's often a lost job. That's exactly where the assistant comes in: it picks up, finds out what the call is about, notes when and how to call back, and instead of a silent voicemail you get a structured note.</p>

## What an AI phone assistant does well
<ul>
<li><strong>Taking requests and passing them on.</strong> Name, callback number, request and urgency, captured cleanly and delivered straight to the right inbox.</li>
<li><strong>Answering the same questions again and again.</strong> Opening hours, directions, who's responsible, status updates. These calls take up a surprising amount of time.</li>
<li><strong>Booking appointments.</strong> With a connected calendar, the assistant can offer available times and enter the appointment directly.</li>
<li><strong>Pre-sorting calls.</strong> Emergency, new customer, existing customer, sales call: the classification happens before the call is passed on.</li>
<li><strong>Working outside business hours.</strong> In the evening, on weekends, during the lunch break.</li>
<li><strong>Handling peaks.</strong> When several people call at once, nobody ends up waiting on hold.</li>
</ul>

## Where the limits are
<p>These points belong in the conversation before anyone expects too much:</p>

<p><strong>Complex expert advice.</strong> Someone who wants to know which of three technical options suits their special case needs a person. The assistant can take the request, not answer it.</p>

<p><strong>Emotional conversations.</strong> An upset customer after a wrong delivery should talk to a person. Good systems recognize this and hand over instead of trying to handle it themselves.</p>

<p><strong>Strong accents and poor connections.</strong> Speech recognition has become very good, but it isn't infallible. Calls from noisy environments or with a strong regional accent can cause misunderstandings.</p>

<p><strong>Binding commitments.</strong> Prices, appointments with contractual weight, technical feasibility: no automated system should commit to these. Draw that line deliberately.</p>

<div class="keypoint">
<p><strong>The rule of thumb:</strong> an AI phone assistant is good at taking in and organizing requests. It's poor at resolving them for good. If you see it as a better front desk rather than a replacement for skilled staff, you'll be happy with it.</p>
</div>

## Which businesses it suits
<p>It makes particular sense if several of these points apply:</p>

<ul>
<li>People regularly call when nobody can pick up.</li>
<li>A large share of calls are about the same questions.</li>
<li>Calls outside business hours are common and could bring in revenue.</li>
<li>There's no dedicated receptionist.</li>
<li>The first contact decides whether you win the job, which is typical for trades and service businesses.</li>
</ul>

<p>It makes less sense with very low call volumes, when most first calls are complex, or when someone is always available to answer the phone anyway.</p>

## Data protection: what to keep in mind
<p>Calls with an AI system involve personal data, sometimes recorded or transcribed. Three points are mandatory:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Transparency.</strong> Callers must be told at the start that they're speaking to an automated system, and they must be able to reach a person.</li>
<li><strong>A data processing agreement</strong> with the speech processing provider, including clarity on where the data is processed. For many businesses, processing within the EU is the simplest answer.</li>
<li><strong>A deletion policy.</strong> How long are recordings and transcripts kept? This question should be answered before the system goes live, not afterwards.</li>
</ol>

## How the setup works
<p>The technical effort is manageable; the content work is what determines the result:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Collect the reasons people call.</strong> For two weeks, note down what calls are actually about. This list is the foundation for everything else.</li>
<li><strong>Define the call flow.</strong> What does the assistant ask, in what order, and when does it hand over to a person?</li>
<li><strong>Set up the integrations.</strong> Phone system, calendar, inbox or ticket system.</li>
<li><strong>Test with real calls,</strong> initially outside core hours. The first conversations reliably show what's missing from the call flow.</li>
<li><strong>Refine.</strong> After two to four weeks, review the logs and fix the most common missteps.</li>
</ol>

<p>The first step is often skipped, yet it's the most important one. An assistant built on guesses about why people call will miss reality.</p>

## What it costs
<p>Pricing usually consists of a base fee plus a usage-based component per call minute, along with a one-time setup. The right benchmark is rarely a receptionist's salary; it's the jobs lost to unanswered calls. If you can estimate how many calls go unanswered each week and what an average job is worth, the math is done quickly.</p>
